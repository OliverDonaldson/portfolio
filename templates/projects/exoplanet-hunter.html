Exoplanet Hunter

Detecting transiting exoplanets in NASA TESS & Kepler light curves with a calibrated dual-view 1D CNN.

Project Overview

Exoplanet Hunter is an end-to-end deep-learning pipeline that decides whether the brightness dips in unreviewed NASA TESS and Kepler targets are genuine planetary transits or false positives — and, crucially, ranks them by a calibrated probability so the most promising candidates can be prioritised for human follow-up. It was a self-directed research project I built to bridge my software-engineering background with my degree in data science and a long-standing interest in astronomy.

The whole pipeline runs on real data only — no synthetic light curves in the final training set. It downloads stitched light curves directly from the Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes (MAST) via lightkurve, cleans, flattens and phase-folds them, builds a dual-view (global + local) representation, and feeds it through a 1D convolutional neural network with attention. The output is a probability, an uncertainty estimate, and a six-panel vetting figure for every target.

The core question: "Can a deep-learning model trained on real NASA TESS and Kepler data actually identify exoplanet transits — and what would it take to build one?"

The Engineering Pivot

My original "dream goal" was a blind transit search that would survey the sky and discover entirely new planets from scratch. Digging into the astrophysics and the data reality reshaped that ambition, and the honest account of why is one of the parts of this project I'm most proud of:

  • Labels are a hard prerequisite. A supervised CNN can't learn what a transit looks like without a clean catalogue of confirmed positives and documented false positives. After group-stratifying by host star and filtering for usable signal, the labelled pool collapses to ~3,275 usable training rows.
  • Blind search is compute-infeasible on a laptop. A from-scratch Box-Least-Squares sweep over a single TESS cycle works out to roughly 1,100 years of sequential compute on one MacBook — discovery is a downstream application of a classifier, not something you can train directly toward.
  • The field's real bottleneck is vetting, not detection. As of May 2026 TESS alone has produced ~7,931 candidates, of which only ~885 are confirmed; roughly 7,000 sit unreviewed in a queue that already exceeds human vetting capacity. The problem isn't finding more anomalies — it's sorting the ones we already have.

So I re-scoped the project to calibrated candidate ranking — exactly the output an over-stretched follow-up reviewer needs: trustworthy probabilities and uncertainty estimates that surface the highest-confidence targets and demote obvious false positives.

How It Works

1. Data & Preprocessing

Light curves are pulled from MAST, stitched, cleaned and flattened, then phase-folded into the dual-view (global + local) representation introduced by Shallue & Vanderburg — a wide view of the whole orbit plus a zoomed-in view of the transit window.

2. The Model

A 1D CNN with Squeeze-and-Excitation channel attention, bilateral multi-head attention with residual late-fusion, and a Wide & Deep auxiliary path that carries nine stellar / transit / centroid-shift features alongside the light-curve towers. A classical Random Forest on hand-crafted features provides a baseline reference.

3. Calibration & Uncertainty

Raw neural networks are overconfident, which makes their probabilities unsafe for ranking. I added post-hoc temperature scaling (fitted per fold) so the outputs reflect true likelihoods, plus MC-Dropout to attach an uncertainty estimate to every prediction.

4. Reproducible MLOps

Everything is composed with Hydra configs and tracked in MLflow — every hyperparameter, metric, plot and artefact is version-controlled, and every dataset is rebuildable from the catalogue queries. Training uses 5-fold StratifiedGroupKFold grouped by host star so multi-planet systems can never leak across the train/test boundary and inflate the scores.

Results

Across 5-fold group-stratified cross-validation on 3,275 TESS + Kepler targets, the final model reaches performance at parity with a recently published peer-reviewed model (Islam's ExoNet, ROC-AUC 0.955) on a comparable dataset:

Every fold clears 0.948 ROC-AUC. The largest single discrimination gain came from the Squeeze-and-Excitation + multi-head-attention + residual-fusion block (+0.040 ROC), and the largest calibration gain from a log1p-transformed centroid-shift feature for distinguishing background eclipsing binaries. Applied to the backlog, the pipeline produces a ranked priority list of ~6,200 unconfirmed candidates, with 146 high-confidence picks (probability ≥ 0.95).

What the Model Attends To

Rather than treating the attention block as a black box, I re-ran the multi-head attention at inference to see what it keys on. The robust, repeatable result is that the global tower's attention localises sharply onto the transit phase — every query position attends preferentially to the transit, regardless of where it sits in the orbit, and this holds even for a 703-day candidate. That's the clearest interpretability evidence that the model is keying on the physically meaningful part of the signal rather than out-of-transit noise. I report two honest null results alongside it (the local high-resolution tower and the SE channel gates show no comparable structure), because being transparent about what didn't work mattered to me as much as the headline numbers.

Development & AI Collaboration

I built the project in VS Code using Anthropic's Claude (via the Claude Code CLI) as an in-terminal pair-programming and tutoring collaborator. The architecture was deliberately learning-first: as a data-science student rather than an astronomer, I used Claude as an intellectual sounding board and scaffolding tool, then took rigorous ownership of the pipeline — verifying every design choice, reading the peer-reviewed papers behind each component, and refining the system through continuous independent research. The vision, technical execution, judgement and final synthesis are entirely mine; the full methodology, references and limitations are documented in the research report linked above.

Reflection

This project pushed me well beyond textbook machine learning into the friction of real, un-sanitised scientific data. I implemented leakage-safe StratifiedGroupKFold validation, engineered probability-calibration pipelines so the outputs are mathematically trustworthy, moved from loose scripts to reproducible Hydra + MLflow experiment tracking, and had to understand the actual physics — gravity darkening, transit-duration variation and third-light contamination — that separates real stellar signals from clean textbook examples. Most of all it taught me to scope an ambitious idea down to a tractable, genuinely useful problem, and to be honest in writing about what the system can and can't do.
